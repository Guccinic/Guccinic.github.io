About

I work in health informatics, on the boundary between clinical systems and the compliance regimes that govern them. My current research began from a narrow observation: almost every healthcare organization in the United States holds exactly one mandatory information-security obligation, and it governs access to data rather than the behavior of the software making decisions about patients.

That gap turns out to be structural rather than accidental. Obligation drives implementation; the obligation stops at the data layer; and so the layer where artificial intelligence actually creates risk goes unmonitored — not through negligence, but because nothing requires it and the frameworks that would cover it cost six figures to certify against.

The work that follows from that observation is less about policy than about plumbing: if the audit infrastructure is already legally required and already funded, the question is why it has never been pointed at AI, and what it would take to do so at an organization that cannot afford a certification program.
