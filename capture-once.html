Section 01

Introduction

What this article argues, who it is written for, and how its claims are categorized.

Artificial intelligence is now a routine part of how many American healthcare organizations operate. Tools that flag sepsis risk, suggest diagnostic codes, screen radiology images, pre-authorize procedures, and document clinical encounters are in use across hospitals, health plans, physician practices, and pharmacies. Most were deployed without a common standard for monitoring their ongoing performance. This article examines why that gap exists and proposes a practical way to close it.

To understand the problem, it helps to first understand the regulatory environment. Most American healthcare organizations are legally required to comply with one federal law — the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act, known as HIPAA. HIPAA is the compliance floor for healthcare. Stronger frameworks exist and are widely recognized in the industry, but they are voluntary and expensive to certify against. Because compliance spending in healthcare is almost entirely obligation-driven, most organizations stay at the floor — and that floor was designed long before AI became clinically significant.

This creates a structural gap: the aspects of an AI system that carry the most risk — whether a model's predictions have grown less reliable over time, whether clinicians are acting on its output, whether it performs differently for different patient populations — fall largely outside what any mandatory compliance framework currently addresses. Not because organizations are careless, but because the obligations they hold simply do not reach that part of the stack.

This article proposes that the infrastructure for AI monitoring already exists inside every HIPAA-compliant organization — in the form of audit logging that federal law already requires. It simply has never been pointed at AI.

The proposed approach, called “Capture Once, Govern Many,” standardizes the form of an AI interaction record and then reads that record through multiple compliance lenses. This means the same captured data can satisfy governance requirements across several frameworks without rebuilding the collection infrastructure for each. The architecture is designed to scale down as well as up — to be viable for a critical access hospital with no dedicated data team, not only for a large health system with a compliance department.

This article is written for readers who are not already familiar with healthcare compliance frameworks or AI monitoring systems. Technical terms are explained when they first appear. The claims made throughout fall into three categories, which are labeled explicitly where it matters:

  • Documented — traceable to a published regulation, standard, dataset, or peer-reviewed study.
  • Inferred — a reasoned conclusion drawn from documented facts, where the reasoning rather than the facts themselves might be contested.
  • Hypothesis — an assertion made without direct supporting evidence, stated as something testable. Every claim about how the proposed architecture would perform in practice is a hypothesis. No organization has built and evaluated this system yet.
Section 02

The Regulatory Baseline: HIPAA

What HIPAA requires, what it does not, and why a two-decade-old distinction still shapes the security posture of most healthcare organizations today.

What HIPAA Is

The Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act, enacted in 1996, is the primary federal law governing the privacy and security of health information in the United States. Most people are familiar with HIPAA in the context of patient privacy — the requirement that healthcare providers protect the confidentiality of medical records. But HIPAA also includes a Security Rule, finalized in 2003, that sets technical and administrative standards for safeguarding electronic protected health information, or ePHI.

One important point that is frequently misunderstood: there is no such thing as being “HIPAA certified.” HIPAA is a legal obligation, not a certification program. Organizations assert that they are in compliance; no independent third party checks this assertion unless a breach occurs or the government initiates an audit. This is fundamentally different from other frameworks discussed later in this article, where compliance is demonstrated through an external audit that produces a certificate.

The Required vs. Addressable Distinction

The Security Rule divides its safeguards into two categories: required specifications, which organizations must implement, and addressable specifications, which organizations must either implement or document a justification for not implementing. In practice, the “addressable” category has functioned as effectively optional for nearly two decades.

Key Term: Addressable

An “addressable” specification under HIPAA's Security Rule means an organization must assess whether the safeguard is reasonable and appropriate given its size, capabilities, and risk profile. If it determines the safeguard is not appropriate, it must document that determination and implement an equivalent alternative. In practice, this standard has allowed organizations to forgo important security controls without implementing meaningful substitutes.

The consequences of this flexibility are significant. Encryption at rest — scrambling stored data so that it is unreadable without a decryption key — and multi-factor authentication (MFA) — requiring users to verify their identity through more than one method, such as a password and a phone notification — have both been classified as addressable. This is why, as of 2026, neither is universal in healthcare, despite being standard practice in virtually every other regulated industry handling sensitive data.

The Proposed Update and Its Current Status

The Department of Health and Human Services proposed removing the required/addressable distinction in December 2024, which would make encryption and multi-factor authentication mandatory across all covered entities. That proposal was published in the Federal Register on January 6, 2025, and drew more than 4,000 public comments. As of this writing, it has not been finalized. The current target for a final rule is July 2027.

This means the weak compliance floor that has characterized healthcare security for twenty years is still in place, and organizations planning around it face another year or more of ambiguity.

The Cost of a Low Baseline

Healthcare has been the most-breached and most expensive-to-breach sector in the United States for fourteen consecutive years. The statistics below reflect conditions as of mid-2026 and illustrate the scope of the problem that a decades-old, weakly enforced security standard has produced. Section 03

The Broader Compliance Landscape

The frameworks that go further than HIPAA, what each one covers, and why the cost of formal certification keeps most healthcare organizations from pursuing them.

A range of compliance frameworks exists beyond HIPAA, each addressing different aspects of information security, system trustworthiness, and, increasingly, the governance of artificial intelligence. Understanding what each framework covers — and what it costs — is essential context for understanding the gap this article addresses.

The Major Frameworks

SOC 2 — Service Organization Control 2

SOC 2 is an auditing framework developed by the American Institute of Certified Public Accountants (AICPA). It evaluates the controls a service organization has in place around five “trust service criteria”: security, availability, processing integrity, confidentiality, and privacy. SOC 2 reports are issued by independent auditors and are widely requested by enterprise customers as a condition of doing business with a software or cloud services vendor.

In healthcare, digital health companies and AI vendors frequently pursue SOC 2 certification because their hospital and health system customers require it contractually. Direct care providers — hospitals, physician practices, home health agencies — are far less likely to certify against SOC 2.

HITRUST — Health Information Trust Alliance

HITRUST, sometimes called the HITRUST CSF (Common Security Framework), is a healthcare-specific security certification that harmonizes requirements from several other frameworks, including HIPAA, SOC 2, ISO 27001, and NIST guidelines. Because it was designed specifically for healthcare, it accounts for the regulatory complexity of the sector and is recognized by regulators and large payers.

HITRUST certification is considered the gold standard of healthcare security compliance. It requires an independent assessment by a HITRUST-authorized evaluator and is significantly more expensive and time-consuming than most other certifications. A small healthcare organization might spend $35,000 to $100,000 on a single HITRUST certification cycle; a large multi-state system has exceeded $500,000. These costs recur on certification renewal cycles of one to two years.

FedRAMP — Federal Risk and Authorization Management Program

FedRAMP is a U.S. government program that certifies cloud service providers for use by federal agencies. It is based on NIST Special Publication 800-53, a comprehensive catalog of security and privacy controls. While FedRAMP is primarily relevant to vendors selling cloud services to the government, its underlying control catalog — NIST 800-53 — is widely used as a reference standard in healthcare, particularly by organizations that have government contracts or that want to demonstrate security rigor beyond what HIPAA requires.

Full FedRAMP authorization is among the most expensive certifications in the compliance landscape, with costs ranging from hundreds of thousands of dollars to over one million.

ISO 42001 — AI Management System

The International Organization for Standardization published ISO 42001 in 2023 as the first international standard for artificial intelligence management systems. It provides a framework for organizations to identify, assess, and mitigate risks associated with AI, covering the full lifecycle from design to decommissioning. As of mid-2026, ISO 42001 is newer and less established in healthcare than the frameworks above, though adoption is growing, particularly among AI developers.

NIST AI RMF — AI Risk Management Framework

The National Institute of Standards and Technology released its AI Risk Management Framework in January 2023. Unlike ISO 42001, the NIST AI RMF is not a certification program — it is a voluntary guidance document organized around four functions: Govern, Map, Measure, and Manage. Healthcare organizations and AI developers use it as a structured approach to thinking about AI risk, but it does not produce a certificate.

Joint Commission RUAIH (Launched June 2026)

The Joint Commission launched the Responsible Use of AI in Healthcare (RUAIH) certification in June 2026. This is a voluntary, fee-based certification available to any U.S. healthcare organization, whether or not it already holds Joint Commission accreditation. RUAIH is organized around five areas, including monitoring, evaluating, and validating AI safety performance.

URAC Health Care AI Accreditation (2025–2026)

URAC introduced a Health Care AI accreditation program in 2025–2026, with separate pathways for AI developers and AI users. The program covers areas including model drift management, ongoing oversight, and testing protocols. The first organizations received URAC AI accreditation in 2026.

The pattern is immediately visible: everything beyond HIPAA is voluntary and costs significant money. Healthcare compliance spending is almost entirely mandate-driven — organizations implement what they are legally required to implement and rarely go beyond it.

Key Insight: Controls vs. Certification

A compliance framework consists of two separable components. The first is a control catalog — a published list specifying what an organization must do. Most of these catalogs are freely available: NIST 800-53 is a public document, the FedRAMP baselines built on it are public, and the NIST AI RMF is freely downloadable. The second is an attestation — a third-party audit confirming that the controls are actually in place. This is where the expense lies.

An organization can implement HITRUST-level controls without paying for a HITRUST certificate. It forfeits the badge, but retains whatever protective effect those controls provide. This distinction is central to the architecture proposed in Section 06.

Section 04

The AI Oversight Gap

What happens when an AI system is deployed inside a healthcare organization, and what each compliance framework does — and does not — address about that deployment.

How an AI System Actually Works in a Healthcare Setting

To understand the compliance gap, it helps to think through what deploying an AI system actually involves. Consider a hospital that adopts a commercial tool that uses artificial intelligence to flag patients at elevated risk for sepsis — a life-threatening response to infection. When a clinician orders labs on an at-risk patient, the AI tool analyzes the results, generates a risk score, and surfaces an alert in the electronic health record (EHR).

This single scenario involves several distinct layers of technology and process:

  • Infrastructure: The servers, cloud environment, or data center where the AI model runs.
  • Data: The patient information the model accesses — lab values, vital signs, prior diagnoses. This is the layer most familiar from HIPAA discussions.
  • Vendor: The company that built and maintains the AI tool and their security controls.
  • The model itself: The AI's ongoing accuracy. A model trained on data from large academic medical centers may perform differently in a rural community hospital. A model that was accurate when deployed may become less accurate over time as patient populations shift — a phenomenon called model drift.
  • Workflow and human response: What clinicians actually do with the AI's output. A model that is technically accurate but consistently overridden has a very different risk profile than one that is heeded.

What Each Framework Actually Governs

When mapped against these layers, a striking pattern emerges: each compliance framework addresses a different slice of a deployed AI system, and the slices do not add up to the whole. (Inferred: this mapping is the article's own analysis.)

An organization holding all of these frameworks would cover essentially the full stack. But an organization holding only HIPAA — which describes the vast majority of direct care providers — covers one slice: data. And the data layer is not where most AI risk lives.

The Gap, Stated Plainly

Organizations required to comply only with HIPAA have no compliance-driven obligation to monitor whether their AI models are still accurate, whether performance has drifted since deployment, whether the AI vendor has changed the underlying model, or how their clinicians are actually responding to AI-generated recommendations.

This is not a statement about those organizations' intentions or diligence. It is a structural observation: when compliance spending follows legal obligation, and legal obligations stop at the data layer, AI governance receives no dedicated resources.

Section 05

The 2026 Certification Wave

Three new programs entered the healthcare AI governance space in a five-week period in mid-2026. What changed, and what a striking gap in all of them reveals about the remaining problem.

Between May and June 2026, the healthcare AI governance landscape changed materially. Three distinct developments — one from an industry coalition, one from the sector's most established accreditation body, and one from a clinical quality organization — introduced new frameworks specifically for AI oversight in healthcare. A peer-reviewed maturity model also appeared in the academic literature. Together, these represent the market's first organized response to the gap described in Section 04.

CHAI Governance Playbooks (May 27, 2026)

The Coalition for Health AI published a comprehensive set of governance playbooks in May 2026, developed with contributions from more than 150 organizations across 100 institutions. The CHAI playbooks cover eight governance elements: AI policy; organizational structures; organizational resources; lifecycle management; risk and impact assessment; data management; third-party management; and education and feedback.

Crucially, the CHAI playbooks are free and publicly available. They represent a control catalog without a certification pathway — precisely the separation between controls and attestation described in Section 03.

Joint Commission RUAIH Certification (June 1, 2026)

The Joint Commission — which accredits approximately 22,000 healthcare organizations in the United States — launched its Responsible Use of AI in Healthcare certification on June 1, 2026. RUAIH is a voluntary, fee-based certification organized around five areas, including one explicitly focused on “monitoring, evaluating, and validating safety performance” of AI systems.

URAC Health Care AI Accreditation (2025–2026)

URAC introduced separate accreditation pathways for AI developers and AI users in 2025–2026. The user pathway covers ongoing oversight practices including model drift management, testing protocols, and maintenance of AI systems in production.

HAIRA: A Peer-Reviewed Maturity Model (February 2026)

In February 2026, a team of researchers published the Healthcare AI Governance Readiness Assessment (HAIRA) in npj Digital Medicine. HAIRA is a five-level governance maturity model spanning seven domains, derived from a systematic review of 35 existing AI governance frameworks.

What This Wave Confirms

The market performed in five weeks what this article's architecture proposes as an ongoing structure: it separated the free control catalog (CHAI's playbooks) from the paid attestation (Joint Commission and URAC certifications built on substantially similar ground).

The Gap That Remains

All three programs specify that organizations must monitor their AI systems. None specifies how the data being monitored is collected. They are requirement layers without an instrumentation layer.

The question has shifted from “no framework exists for AI monitoring” to “four frameworks now specify what to monitor, and none specifies how to capture the underlying data — and the organizations all four aim to serve are precisely the ones least equipped to build their own capture infrastructure.”

Section 06

The Architecture

The core proposal: a standardized event record that every AI interaction writes to once, and a set of compliance lenses that read it differently for each framework.

The Central Idea

The architecture described here rests on an observation about what compliance frameworks and monitoring systems share: they all want to answer questions about the same set of events. HIPAA wants to know who accessed what patient data and when. The NIST AI RMF wants to know whether the model is still performing accurately and whether humans are appropriately overseeing it. The Joint Commission's RUAIH certification wants evidence that an organization is monitoring AI safety performance.

Each of these questions can be answered from a single, well-designed record of what happened during an AI interaction. The proposal is to write that record once and then read it through whichever compliance lenses an organization needs to satisfy. The first lens carries the full cost of capture. Every additional lens is nearly free, because the underlying data already exists.

Pay for capture once. Every lens after the first costs almost nothing — the data already exists.

Why Governance Should Be Architecture, Not Policy

There are two fundamental approaches to enforcing any rule. Policy enforcement writes the rule down, trains people on it, and audits for compliance later. Architectural enforcement makes non-compliant behavior structurally difficult — the rule is built into the system itself. Healthcare AI governance today is almost entirely the first kind, which is why producing audit evidence is a project someone has to initiate rather than a byproduct of the system running normally.

The security field has already made this transition in other domains. Password-sharing policies were replaced by single sign-on and multi-factor authentication. Nobody “complies” with a badge reader — they simply cannot open the door without one. The architecture proposed here applies the same logic to AI monitoring: capture is passive, it happens automatically as the system runs, and the compliance evidence it produces is a byproduct rather than an added task.

The Event Record: What Gets Written Down

Every AI interaction writes one standardized record. The record is designed to be small — it stores references and identifiers, not the actual content of the AI interaction — which keeps it out of scope for most data privacy concerns and makes it far easier to approve within a compliance-conscious organization.

# One record per AI interaction event_id A unique identifier for this specific event timestamp When the interaction occurred actor_id Who initiated it — a clinician, coder, or automated service actor_role Their role: clinician / coder / reviewer / automated subject_ref A reference to the patient, claim, or member — not the data itself encounter_ref The clinical episode this interaction belongs to deployment_id Which AI deployment produced this — model + population + workflow + version purpose Why this AI call was made input_ref A pointer and hash to the input data — again, not the data itself output_summary The AI's output: a score, a classification, a recommended action confidence The model's stated confidence level, if it provides one latency_ms How long the model took to respond egress_target Where data was sent; whether a BAA covers that endpoint human_action What the clinician did: accepted / modified / rejected / ignored action_latency Time elapsed between the AI's output and the human's response artifact_ref A reference to any note, order, denial, or report produced downstream_ref A link to the outcome — filled in later when it becomes known
Three Design Rules That Are Not Optional

Store references, never the underlying data. The record holds pointers and cryptographic hashes — not the patient data itself. This keeps the monitoring layer outside the scope of most privacy regulations.

Use deployment_id, not model_id. The same AI model running in two different settings is two different governed objects. Treating them as a single entity because they share the same underlying model is the most common and consequential error in AI governance design.

Capture human_action. This is the field that almost no current monitoring system records, and it carries some of the most important governance information. A model that clinicians accept 6% of the time and ignore 94% of the time is not performing a clinical function — it is generating alert fatigue.

The Compliance Lenses

A lens is a predefined analysis run over the event record, derived from one framework's control catalog. The method for deriving a lens is straightforward: for each control a framework specifies, ask what observable fact in the event record would demonstrate that control is operating.

The Human lens deserves special attention because it covers a documented harm category that the other lenses structurally cannot see: a model that is technically accurate, but whose presence in a workflow progressively degrades the clinical judgment of the people using it. Research on automation bias is well-established across multiple domains. In healthcare specifically, a 2025 study on endoscopist performance following AI adoption found measurable skill decline among physicians who worked regularly with AI assistance.

Section 07

Collection Methods

Six distinct ways to fill the event record, matched to different organizational budgets, technical capacities, and AI deployment types.

The event record described in Section 06 can be filled through six different collection methods. These methods differ considerably in what they can observe, what they cost, whether they require engineering staff, and whether they work on AI embedded inside a vendor's product — a category that encompasses a large and growing share of the AI healthcare organizations actually use.

Method A — Intercepting the Call

The first method involves inserting a wrapper — sometimes called a shim or middleware — around the AI call itself. A wrapper is a small piece of code that surrounds an existing function and adds logging without modifying the original code. This produces the highest-fidelity record possible: the exact input, the exact output, the precise timing, and the identity of whoever or whatever made the call, all captured in real time.

Method B — Reading What Already Exists

The second method requires no new interception at all. Instead, it reads records that the organization already produces: EHR audit logs, application logs, database change streams, and most importantly, the HL7 and FHIR message traffic flowing through the interface engine that virtually every provider organization operates...
