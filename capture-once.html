Capture Once, Govern Many

Healthcare organizations hold the weakest available compliance obligation because it is the only mandatory one and the alternatives cost six figures to certify. As of mid-2026, four frameworks now specify what AI monitoring must occur — and none specifies how the monitored data is captured. This paper documents that instrumentation gap and proposes an architecture that records each AI interaction once, then applies every framework's control catalog as a view over the same record.

Part 00

Summary of the argument

The full argument in condensed form, followed by its scope and evidentiary limits. Parts 01 through 09 develop, qualify, and attempt to falsify it.

Scope, method, and evidentiary status

What this is. A working paper that (a) documents a coverage gap between healthcare compliance obligations and the operational surface of deployed AI, and (b) proposes an architecture responding to it. It is a proposal supported by a structured review, not a report of findings.

Method. Structured review of federal rulemaking and executive action, compliance framework documentation and published certification cost data, sector breach statistics, and peer-reviewed literature on deployed clinical AI, covering approximately January 2025 through August 2026. Coverage was assessed by mapping each framework's published control scope against the operational layers of a deployed AI system.

What this is not. No primary data were collected. No implementation of the proposed architecture has been built or evaluated. No organization was studied. Every claim about how the architecture would perform in practice is a hypothesis.

Evidentiary convention. Three classes are used throughout and should be read differently:

  • Documented — traceable to a published rule, standard, dataset, or peer-reviewed study. Regulatory timelines, breach statistics, certification costs, and framework scope fall here.
  • Inferred — a reasoned conclusion from documented facts, where the reasoning rather than the facts may be contested. The coverage gap in §1.4 is inferred.
  • Hypothesis — asserted without supporting evidence and stated as testable. Every claim about cost, adoption, and effectiveness of the proposed architecture falls here. The two central hypotheses are stated as Propositions 1 and 2 (§2.3, §3.4) and the falsification conditions are given in §8.

Figures throughout derive from secondary reporting and require primary-source verification before citation. See §9.2.

The situation. Almost every healthcare organization holds exactly one compliance obligation: HIPAA. It is the weakest of the available frameworks — its Security Rule has let encryption and multi-factor authentication remain effectively optional for two decades. Stronger frameworks exist. Nobody is required to hold them, and they cost between $35,000 and over $1,000,000 to certify against. So organizations stay at the floor, and healthcare has been the most-breached and costliest-breached sector for fourteen consecutive years.

The AI complication. Each compliance framework covers a different slice of how an AI system actually runs — infrastructure, vendor, data, model, workflow. HIPAA covers only the data slice, and says nothing about whether a model works, whether it has drifted, or what a human did with its output. An organization holding only HIPAA has no coverage over the part of the stack where AI risk actually lives.

The separable components. A compliance framework consists of two things that are usually purchased together but need not be: a published catalog of controls, which is freely available, and a third-party attestation that those controls are in place, which accounts for the cost. An organization can implement the controls without procuring the attestation. It forfeits the certificate and retains whatever protective effect the controls provide — a proposition examined, and flagged as unmeasured, in §7.4.

The architecture. Every AI interaction is written down as one standard record. Each compliance framework then becomes a lens — a way of reading the same records. HIPAA asks who touched what. A federal-grade lens asks whether the environment drifted. An AI lens asks whether the model still works and whether anyone is overriding it.

You pay for capture once. Every lens after the first is nearly free, because the data already exists.

How the records get written. Six different methods can fill the same record, from intercepting the call directly to simply reading logs the organization is already legally required to keep. Three of the six require no engineering staff and no vendor cooperation. The collection method is a choice matched to budget; the record and the lenses stay identical. That separation is what lets the same architecture serve a national health system and a critical access hospital.

Why it gets adopted. It does not ask anyone to start doing a new thing. In its cheapest form it re-points logging that HIPAA already legally requires. The infrastructure is paid for; it is simply aimed at the wrong target. That is what makes this viable for an organization with no security team and no budget — which is the same organization currently getting hit hardest.

Part 01

The problem, with evidence

Four facts, each independently verifiable. Together they define a gap that is real rather than rhetorical.

1.1 The floor is low on purpose

The HIPAA Security Rule divides its requirements into "required" and "addressable." In practice, addressable has functioned as optional for twenty years. This is why encryption at rest and multi-factor authentication are still not universal in healthcare.

HHS proposed removing that distinction in December 2024 — making encryption and MFA mandatory. That proposal was published in the Federal Register on January 6, 2025, drew more than 4,000 comments, and has not been finalized. The target for a final rule is now July 2027.

So as of today the weak floor is still the floor, and organizations planning around it have another year or more of ambiguity.

This is the part that has not been written down anywhere. Each framework governs a different layer of a running AI system:

Stack all of them and you cover infrastructure → vendor → data → model → workflow → user. Hold only HIPAA and you cover one slice, and it is not the slice where AI risk concentrates.

The gap, stated precisely

Organizations required to hold only HIPAA have no compliance-driven visibility into model behavior, model degradation, vendor AI supply chain, or human response to AI output — because no framework they are obligated to hold covers those things.

This is not a claim that they are careless. It is a structural observation: obligation drives implementation, and their obligations stop at the data layer.

1.5 Why this is a gap and not a complaint

A complaint says people should try harder. A gap says the incentive structure produces a predictable outcome and can be redesigned. Three properties make this a genuine gap:

  • It is structural. The behavior follows from the requirements, not from negligence.
  • It is measurable. Coverage can be mapped framework by framework, layer by layer.
  • It is addressable without new law. The remedy does not depend on a rule being finalized, which matters given the July 2027 target.

1.6 The certification wave of mid-2026 — and what it did not change

Between May and June 2026, three private bodies moved into the space federal policy vacated. This development post-dates the first draft of this paper and materially revises its framing. (Documented.)

  • 27 May 2026 — CHAI governance playbooks. The Coalition for Health AI published control catalogs across eight elements (AI policy; organizational structures; organizational resources; lifecycle management; risk and impact assessment; data management; third-party management; education and feedback), developed with 150+ contributors across 100+ organizations. Free and public.
  • 1 June 2026 — Joint Commission RUAIH certification. A voluntary, fee-based certification open to any U.S. healthcare organization, accredited or not, organized around five areas including "monitoring, evaluating, and validating safety performance."
  • 2025–26 — URAC Health Care AI accreditation. Separate developer and user pathways covering model drift management, testing, and ongoing oversight; first organizations now accredited.

A peer-reviewed maturity model also appeared: HAIRA (Healthcare AI Governance Readiness Assessment; npj Digital Medicine, February 2026), a five-level model across seven domains derived from a systematic review of 35 frameworks, explicitly motivated by the observation that existing frameworks "assume extensive resources, creating barriers for smaller healthcare organizations."

What this wave confirms

Section 2.2 of this paper argues that a compliance framework decomposes into a free control catalog and a paid attestation, and that the two are separable. In a five-week window, the market performed that separation in public: one body published the free catalog (CHAI) while two others launched paid attestations over substantially the same ground (Joint Commission, URAC). A claim that was theoretical in the first draft is now an observed market event with dates.

What this wave does not provide

All three programs — and HAIRA's monitoring-and-maintenance domain — specify that organizations must monitor. None specifies how the monitored data is captured. They are requirement layers without an instrumentation layer. The gap this paper addresses therefore narrows but does not close: it moves from "no framework exists" (no longer true) to "four frameworks now specify what to monitor and none specifies how to capture it — and the organizations all four claim to serve are precisely those least able to build capture."

Part 02

The thesis

Four ideas. The first three are arguments; the fourth is the consequence you are obligated to state out loud.

2.1 Governance is architecture, not policy

There are two ways to enforce any rule. Policy enforcement writes it down, trains people, and audits later. Architectural enforcement makes the wrong behavior structurally difficult.

Healthcare AI governance today is almost entirely the first kind, which is why audit trails are a project someone has to go do rather than a byproduct of the system running. The security field already made this transition — from "do not share your password" to single sign-on and MFA. Nobody complies with a badge reader. They simply cannot get in without one.

2.2 Controls are free; certification is what costs money

This is the economic core, and it is frequently misunderstood even by people who work in compliance.

A framework consists of a control catalog — a published, public list of what you must do — and an attestation, a third-party audit that proves you did it. NIST 800-53 is a free public document. The FedRAMP baselines built on it are public. ISO catalogs are purchasable for a nominal fee.

You do not pay an assessor to implement a control. You pay them to vouch for it.

An organization that cannot afford HITRUST can still implement HITRUST-grade controls. It forfeits the badge and keeps the protection.

2.3 Compliance already built the infrastructure

HIPAA's Security Rule requires audit controls — mechanisms that record and examine activity in systems containing electronic protected health information. In plain terms: you are already legally required to log who did what, to which patient's data, and when.

Now consider what an AI monitoring system needs: who, what, when, which patient, which model, what happened next.

It is very nearly the same data. The audit infrastructure AI governance requires has been mandatory for two decades. It has simply never been pointed at AI.

Proposition 1 — testable

If audit infrastructure mandated under the HIPAA Security Rule is sufficient to answer governance questions about AI, then the marginal cost of AI monitoring at an organization already meeting HIPAA obligations is substantially lower than currently assumed by that organization.

Test: at a sample of organizations, attempt to answer a defined set of AI governance questions using only existing audit log and interface message data. Record how many are answerable, and what specifically blocks the rest. A negative result — that existing logs are insufficient — would falsify the central economic claim of this document and is a plausible outcome. See §3.3 and §8.

2.4 Capture once, govern many

One wrapper writes one event stream. Each framework becomes a lens over that same stream. The first lens carries the full cost of instrumentation; the fifth lens costs almost nothing, because the data is already being captured.

This is why the approach scales down rather than only up — and scaling down is the hard direction that nothing else in this market does.

2.5 The consequence you must state

Local evaluation capacity in healthcare tracks with system membership and operating margin. Well-resourced organizations validate their AI. Under-resourced ones deploy the same models to populations that were often absent from the training data, with nobody checking.

Deregulation does not eliminate the safety burden. It means-tests it.

Any framework that only works at organizations with a data science team reproduces that divide. The tiering in Part 05 is not a nicety — it is the difference between a solution and a market segment.

Part 03

The architecture

Concrete enough to build from. Terms defined as they appear.

3.1 Two fixed parts, one pluggable part

The architecture has exactly two things that must not vary, and one that must:

  • Fixed — the event record. The standard shape of "an AI thing happened."
  • Fixed — the lenses. Compliance views computed over those records.
  • Pluggable — the collector. However the record gets filled in.
The design decision that makes this work

It is tempting to treat interception as the architecture. It is not. Interception is one collector, it requires engineering staff, and it structurally cannot reach AI embedded inside a vendor's product — which is a large share of the AI that matters.

Separating the collector from the record is what lets the same architecture serve a national system and a twenty-five-bed rural hospital. It is also what prevents an EHR vendor from making the whole approach irrelevant by shipping their own version: they can own one collector; they cannot own the record or the lenses across every vendor an organization uses.

3.2 The event — the thing everything else reads

Every AI interaction emits one record. This schema is the single most important artifact in the whole design, because every lens is a query against it.

# one record per AI interaction event_id unique id timestamp when actor_id who invoked it (person or service) actor_role clinician / coder / reviewer / automated subject_ref patient or claim or member reference encounter_ref the episode this belongs to deployment_id model + population + workflow + version purpose why this call was made input_ref pointer + hash (never the payload itself) output_summary score / class / action recommended confidence if the model exposes one latency_ms performance egress_target where data went; BAA status of that endpoint human_action accepted / modified / rejected / ignored action_latency time from output to human action artifact_ref note, order, denial, report produced downstream_ref outcome linkage, filled in later
Three design rules that are not negotiable
  • Store references, never payloads. The log holds pointers and hashes, not patient data. This keeps the monitoring layer out of scope for a great deal of risk and makes it far easier to approve.
  • deployment_id, not model_id. The same model in two settings is two governed objects with separate baselines and separate retirement dates. Collapsing them is the most common and most consequential modeling error in this space.
  • human_action is mandatory. It is the field nobody captures and the one that carries the most information. A model with a 94% override rate is not performing well; it is furniture, and it is still producing alert fatigue.

3.3 The six collectors

Six distinct methods can fill the event record. They differ in what they can see, what they cost, and whether they work on AI the organization does not control.

A · Intercept the call

A wrapper (also called a shim or middleware) is a small piece of code that sits around an existing function to add logging — without opening or modifying the original. Highest fidelity: input, output, timing, requester, all in real time.

Constraint: requires engineering staff and a call path you control. Cannot reach AI running entirely inside a vendor's product.

B · Read what is already written

Intercept nothing. Read the records the organization already produces: EHR audit logs, application logs, database change streams, and — most importantly in healthcare — the HL7 and FHIR message traffic already flowing through the interface engine that virtually every provider organization runs.

This is the most thesis-consistent collector available. HIPAA already mandates the audit logs. Federal interoperability rules already mandate FHIR access, and the current policy direction is toward more standardized API access, not less. Nothing new is built; an existing, legally required pipe is read.

Constraint: sees artifacts and access, not the model call itself. And healthcare log quality is genuinely poor — see the caveat below.

C · Instrument the interface

Capture at the point of use — an in-EHR application, browser-level instrumentation, or session telemetry. The only collector that reliably observes what a person actually saw and did.

Constraint: moderate cost and a real consent and trust burden. Politically the most sensitive of the six.

D · Test from outside — proficiency testing for algorithms

Observe no real traffic at all. On a schedule, submit cases whose correct answer is already known and record what comes back.

The precedent that makes this immediately legible

Clinical laboratories are legally required under CLIA to do exactly this — periodically run specimens with known values through their instruments to demonstrate continued accuracy. It is called proficiency testing, it has been standard practice for decades, and no laboratorian needs it explained.

Nobody does it for algorithms. Proposing it is not proposing a new concept; it is proposing an accepted quality mechanism for a new object. It requires no integration, no vendor cooperation, no model access, and almost no money — which makes it the only high-value collector genuinely available to an organization with zero technical staff.

Constraint: says nothing about real usage or human behavior. Detects capability decay, not deployment failure.

E · Compare against a reference

Run a known-good model in parallel and record divergence. Detects drift in a production model without opening either one.

Constraint: needs compute and a defensible reference. Justified for high-volume, high-stakes deployments only.

F · Ask

Scheduled attestations, a reporting channel for anomalous AI output, and periodic random review of AI-touched records.

The weakest signal and the cheapest — but the only one that catches semantic failure: output that is fluent, plausible, well-formed, and wrong. No instrumentation will ever find that. An AI incident-reporting channel is startlingly rare in practice and is the closest available analog to how drug safety monitoring actually works.

Interception — the highest-fidelity option — works in the fewest situations and costs the most. Any framework that assumes interception has quietly restricted itself to well-resourced organizations governing only the AI they built themselves.

The honest caveat on Collector B

Healthcare audit logs are frequently incomplete, inconsistently formatted, and retained for varying periods. "HIPAA requires the logs" and "the logs are usable" are different claims, and the gap between them is the single largest execution risk in the cheap tier.

Test this before designing around it. Pull thirty days of audit log from one system and try to answer one governance question. That exercise costs a day and determines whether Collector B is viable at that organization.

3.4 Collector tiering

Collectors are not competitors. They are a portfolio selected against budget and technical capacity. The tiering below is the default recommendation.

C0

Day one, any organization — B + D + F

Read the logs and message streams that already exist. Run proficiency tests against the top three deployments. Open an AI incident reporting channel.

No engineering staff. No vendor cooperation. No new infrastructure. This tier alone produces HIPAA audit evidence, capability-decay detection, and a semantic failure channel — three things most large health systems currently lack.

C1

With an engineering team — add A

Interception on systems the organization actually controls: internally built tools, API-based services, agents. Adds real-time call visibility and confidence data where it is obtainable.

C2

Where stakes justify it — add C or E

Interface instrumentation where human behavior is the risk. Reference-model comparison where drift in a high-volume deployment would be consequential.

Neither is a general requirement. Both are expensive enough that they should be triggered by a specific named risk, not adopted by default.

Proposition 2 — testable, and the strongest claim made here

The equity argument in §2.5 holds only if the lowest collector tier produces non-trivial detection. If C0 amounts to record-keeping, the document concedes that meaningful governance requires capital.

Claim: an organization running only C0 collectors detects classes of failure — capability decay via proficiency testing, semantic error via incident reporting — that a well-resourced organization running a conventional model-monitoring platform does not currently detect, because conventional platforms monitor model outputs rather than model capability or human-observed error.

Test: instrument one small and one large organization; compare the failure classes each surfaces over a fixed period. If the small site surfaces failure classes the large site misses, the expected relationship between resources and safety is at least partially inverted. This is a strong claim and is currently unevidenced.

3.5 The lens model

A lens is a read-only analysis over the event record, derived from one framework's control catalog. Lenses share the record and never share code paths, so a new one can be added without touching collection.

How a lens is derived — the rule, so this is a method and not a list

For each control in a framework's catalog, ask: what observable fact in the event record would demonstrate this control is operating? If an answer exists, that control joins a lens. If none exists, the control is out of scope and must be stated as out of scope.

This matters because it makes coverage auditable rather than asserted. A buyer can be handed the catalog, the mapping, and the list of controls deliberately excluded — which is a far stronger position than claiming alignment in the abstract.

Lenses are not equally mature. The first four below are direct translations of existing control catalogs. The last three are constructions, and should be labeled as such.

The mid-2026 catalogs are new lens sources. The CHAI playbooks' eight elements and the Joint Commission certification's five standard areas are both derivable as lenses under the rule above — and because the CHAI catalogs are free and public, they are the natural first healthcare-native derivation alongside HIPAA. An organization pursuing certification then holds a running head start: the monitoring evidence the certifier asks for is a query against records already being captured.

The Human lens deserves special attention because no compliance framework requires it and it captures a documented harm the others structurally cannot see: the model performs correctly and the people using it degrade. This is the most original component of the design.

Design constraint on the Human lens

Monitor and alert at the cohort level. Never surface individual scorecards. The moment this becomes a performance management tool, the people being measured will block it, game it, or grieve it — and you will have destroyed the signal in order to build a dashboard nobody trusts.

Frame it as device surveillance, not employee surveillance. You are measuring what the tool does to practice, not who is underperforming.

3.6 Where it runs

Two deployment patterns, and the vocabulary matters because using the wrong term will get you dismissed by technical buyers.

Air-gapped means nothing enters or leaves — ever. No internet, no updates; model weights arrive on encrypted media with chain of custody. Real, but rarely what anyone actually wants, because results need to get out.

Controlled egress means data goes in and stays in, the model runs inside with no outbound connection, and only reviewed outputs leave. This is what almost every regulated deployment actually is, and it is the correct pattern here.

Precedent worth citing

The CMS Virtual Research Data Center already operates this way: researchers analyze data inside a CMS-managed environment, beneficiary-identifiable data never leaves, and only aggregate output exits after a disclosure review.

You are not proposing an unproven architecture. You are proposing that a pattern the federal government already validated should be standard, with AI governance inside it.

3.7 What the architecture cannot do

Vendor-embedded AI — the residual gap

Collectors B, D and F reach vendor AI far better than interception does, but coverage is still partial: proficiency testing shows whether a vendor model still performs, and log reading shows what it produced, while neither reveals the call itself or a confidence value.

The remainder is a contracting problem, not an engineering one. Require an attested telemetry feed as a condition of purchase, and make "provides neither an interceptable path, a readable artifact trail, nor a telemetry feed" a disqualifying property in procurement. When something cannot be solved in code, solve it in the contract — and let the architecture dictate the contract term.

  • It cannot see inside a model. No explainability, no weights, no training data. By design.
  • It cannot establish causation. Correlated change is not caused change; say so before a reviewer does.
  • Outcome labels lag. Near-term you are measuring process, not outcomes.
  • Generative output evaluation is unsolved. There is no ground truth for a clinical note. Edit distance and omission rate are proxies, not validation — and this is where the highest-volume AI lives. Do not overclaim here; it is the fastest way to lose credibility with a technical audience.
  • It must fail open. A monitoring layer that takes down clinical systems during an outage will be removed within a week, correctly. Which means it cannot double as a hard availability control.
Part 04

Compliance: what you may claim

The single most dangerous section. Overstating here is how this concept gets someone sued.

Claims that are factually incorrect
  • "HIPAA certified." No such thing exists. Anyone who says it identifies themselves as uninformed.
  • "FedRAMP compliant." FedRAMP is an authorization granted through a specific government process. You are either authorized or you are not.
  • "HITRUST certified" without a certificate. This is a trademarked program with a defined assessment.
  • "This makes you compliant." Compliance is an organizational state involving policy, training, contracts, and physical safeguards. Software is one input.

The defensible formulation

"Implements controls aligned to [framework]." Accurate, defensible, and still valuable. You can go further and publish a control mapping — which control, which lens, what evidence it produces — so a buyer can verify the claim themselves.

4.2 The objection that will come from legal

The following is the most consequential objection raised against monitoring systems of this kind, and it is raised by legal counsel rather than by technical staff:

"You are creating discoverable evidence against us."

It is a real concern. A monitoring system that documents a model performing poorly, in an organization that then did nothing, produces the plaintiff's exhibit.

The honest answer has three parts. First, it cuts both ways: documented monitoring with documented response is the strongest available evidence that an organization exercised reasonable care. Second, the alternative is not "no evidence" — it is "no evidence that you were careful," in an environment where AI-related standards of care are actively forming. Third, published guidance from the Joint Commission and CHAI notes that adopting responsible-use recommendations may reduce exposure to claims of negligent implementation.

Design implication: the value is not in detection, it is in documented response. Build the response workflow, escalation path, and closure record as first-class features, not as an afterthought. A finding with no recorded resolution is a liability. A finding with one is a defense.

4.3 State law is now the moving part

With federal transparency requirements under active repeal, state obligations are where new duties are appearing — disclosure of AI use to patients, requirements that a licensed professional make final medical-necessity determinations, and similar. These vary by state and change annually.

Architecturally this argues for a jurisdiction lens: the same event stream, filtered by the rules of the state a given encounter occurred in. Build the state rules as configuration rather than as code, because they will change faster than your release cycle.

Part 05

Implementation

Maturity tiers, build order, and the roles required to actually run it.

5.1 Maturity tiers

Each tier is independently useful; nobody has to reach Tier 4 to benefit.

Two tierings, two different axes — do not confuse them

Collector tiers (C0–C2, Part 03) describe how events get captured. They are chosen by technical capacity.

Maturity tiers (1–4 and S, below) describe how far governance has progressed. They are chosen by organizational readiness.

An organization can sit at maturity Tier 4 while running only C0 collectors. That combination — full response workflow, cheap collection — is arguably the best value available and is the configuration to aim a small organization at.

1

Inventory — any organization, any budget

A list of every AI in use, expressed as deployments rather than products. Which model, which population, which workflow, which owner, when last reviewed.

Can be a spreadsheet. Typically reveals two to three times more AI than leadership believed existed, which is itself the finding that funds Tier 2.

2

Capture

Events are being written for the highest-volume deployments. Start at collector tier C0 — read existing logs and message traffic, run proficiency tests, open an incident channel. Nothing is blocked; everything is recorded.

At this point the Access and Boundary lenses work, which means the organization is producing HIPAA audit evidence it previously could not produce — and the Model lens has a capability signal from proficiency testing without any integration at all.

3

Lenses

Model, Integrity, Environment, and Human lenses running on the captured stream. Deployment reports generate themselves on a schedule.

This is where an organization stops answering governance questions with opinion.

4

Response

Thresholds trigger defined action: flag, review, downgrade to advisory-only, suspend. Every finding carries a recorded resolution.

This is the tier that converts monitoring into governance, and the tier that satisfies legal.

S

Shared / federated service

Small organizations emit events to a shared evaluation service and receive analysis back. Nobody needs local data science staff.

The structural argument for this tier: detecting change in human performance requires more volume than a single small organization has. It must be pooled to work at all. And the pooled infrastructure that makes it statistically possible is the same infrastructure that gives a small provider validation capacity it could never staff. One shared service, two otherwise unsolvable problems.

5.2 Build order

The common failure is starting with a dashboard, because dashboards demonstrate well. You then discover the dashboard cannot answer anything, because nothing was linked. Start with the linkage.

  1. One deployment, full event capture. Prove the record can be written.
  2. Deployment inventory. Enumerate what exists.
  3. C0 collectors in monitor-only mode. Record everything, block nothing. This buys trust and reveals real usage patterns — including AI nobody disclosed.
  4. Access and Boundary lenses. Fastest value; produces compliance evidence immediately.
  5. Model and Human lenses. Requires a baseline period before findings mean anything.
  6. Response workflow. Findings get owners, deadlines, and recorded closure.
  7. Enforcement. Only now. This is a political act, not a technical one.

Steps 3 through 7 form the arc that matters: arrive with evidence before you ask for authority. A control proposed as a control is a turf war. A system that has quietly logged for six months and can show leadership their actual AI footprint is an obvious next step.

5.3 Roles required

These are functions, not headcount. In a small organization one person holds several.

Accountable

Executive owner

Holds the authority to suspend a deployment. Without this the system produces findings nobody can act on. Often a CMIO, CIO, or chief quality officer.

Design

Informatics lead

Owns the event schema and the deployment registry. The only role that sees both the clinical workflow and the data plumbing. This is the keystone role.

Build

Platform engineer

Implements the wrapper, the event pipeline, and the lens queries. Does not need machine-learning expertise; needs integration and observability skill.

Evaluate

Analyst / data scientist

Sets baselines, defines thresholds, interprets drift. At Tier S this role is provided by the shared service rather than hired.

Interpret

Clinical or domain lead

Decides what constitutes a meaningful change in the domain. Prevents statistically real, clinically irrelevant findings from consuming attention.

Constrain

Compliance / privacy officer

Owns control mapping and the claims language. Also owns the discoverability question from Part 04.

Adopt

AI enablement lead

The role most often missing. Owns rollout, training, and the feedback loop from users. Translates findings into workflow change rather than into memos.

Procure

Contracting / vendor management

Turns architectural requirements into contract terms — telemetry access, notification of model changes, right to audit.

5.4 Adoption

The reason this can be adopted "naturally," in your framing, is that it asks almost nobody to change behavior. Capture is passive. Clinicians do not fill anything in. The change management burden falls on a small number of technical and governance staff rather than on the whole workforce — which is the opposite of every governance program that failed before it.

Three adoption rules:

  • Never launch with enforcement. Trust first, control later.
  • Give the first report to the people being measured, before it goes to leadership. This single sequencing decision determines whether clinical staff experience the system as a tool or as a threat.
  • Treat unsanctioned AI use as a demand signal, not a violation. A high rate of blocked calls to consumer tools is a procurement requirement writing itself. Punish it and it goes underground; measure it and it tells you what to buy.

5.5 Failure modes

  • Alert fatigue in the governance layer. A system that fires constantly gets muted like every other alarm. Threshold discipline is a first-class design problem.
  • Findings with no owner. Detection without a response workflow creates liability rather than safety.
  • Scope creep into security tooling. This is not a SIEM and it is not an intrusion detection system. Adjacent, cheaper, better-funded products will eat it if the boundary is unclear.
  • The dashboard trap. Building visualization before linkage.
  • Individual attribution. Covered in Part 03; it is the fastest way to lose clinical cooperation permanently.

Observation on segment ordering

Organizations selling into healthcare — digital health companies, AI vendors, RCM firms — are contractually required to hold SOC 2 or HITRUST because their customers demand it. They feel the certification cost directly and repeatedly.

This segment differs from provider organizations in three respects relevant to early adoption: an existing budget line for compliance evidence, engineering capacity sufficient for interception-based collection, and no restriction on being named publicly. Provider organizations — where the equity argument in §2.5 actually applies — have none of these. Any staged adoption sequence therefore begins with the segment furthest from the stated motivation, which is a tension worth naming rather than resolving.

Part 07

Adoption analysis

Whether an architecture is sound and whether it will be adopted are separate questions with separate evidence. This part examines the second, including the reasons it may not be.

7.1 Why does this not already exist?

This is the first question a serious reader asks, and a proposal that cannot answer it is usually naive rather than novel. If the components are cheap, the substrate is already mandated, and the need is obvious, the absence of an existing product demands an explanation.

There are six, and they are not equally flattering.

  1. The buyer is fragmented. Compliance owns audit evidence. Security owns logs. Quality owns outcomes. IT owns systems. The value spans all four and no single budget covers it. This is the classic profile of a product that should exist and does not.
  2. There is no forcing function. Healthcare compliance spending follows mandates almost exclusively. Nothing here is mandated, which is the entire premise and also the reason nobody has built it.
  3. The enabling substrate is worse than it looks on paper. "HIPAA requires audit logs" is true. "Those logs are complete, consistent, and joinable" is frequently false. Anyone who scoped this work by reading the regulation rather than the logs would have quit at first contact.
  4. The win is invisible. Nobody is promoted for the incident that did not occur. Preventive infrastructure is chronically underfunded in every industry and healthcare is not an exception.
  5. Adjacent markets pay better. The engineering talent capable of building this is currently building fraud detection, revenue cycle automation, and compliance certification tooling, all of which have existing budget lines.
  6. The problem is genuinely new. Until roughly 2023, most clinical AI was a small number of narrow predictive models living inside the EHR, governable by committee review. Generative AI in workflow, ambient documentation at scale, agents, and unsanctioned consumer tool use are recent. The conditions that make this necessary are about three years old — and the market has now begun responding: the May–June 2026 certification wave (§1.6) is evidence that the demand became legible to institutions within the past year. That wave occupied the requirement layer first because requirements are cheaper to produce than instrumentation, which is consistent with, rather than contrary to, the gap this paper identifies.
Assessment

Reasons 1, 2, 4 and 5 are economic and describe a market failure rather than a design flaw — they predict slow adoption regardless of technical merit. Reason 3 is a technical precondition that determines whether the approach functions at all, and is cheaply testable. Reason 6 is the substantive explanation: the conditions creating the need are recent enough that absence of a response is expected rather than evidence of infeasibility.

Distinguishing these matters analytically. A market failure and an infeasibility produce the same observation — nothing exists — and have opposite implications.

7.3 Who is already in the room

  • Healthcare GRC platforms — compliance workflow and risk assessment. Strong on paperwork, weak on runtime behavior.
  • Compliance automation tools — evidence collection for SOC 2 and ISO. Strong on certification, not healthcare-clinical or AI-specific.
  • ML observability vendors — drift and performance monitoring. Strong technically, no compliance mapping, no clinical workflow, no human-behavior layer.
  • Enterprise AI governance suites — inventory and regulation mapping. Generic; retrofitted to healthcare.
  • Academic systems such as Vanderbilt's algorithmovigilance work — the closest published implementation prior art, designed as monitoring alongside health IT rather than as collection in the call path.
  • HAIRA maturity model (npj Digital Medicine, Feb 2026) — the closest published conceptual prior art, and it substantially anticipates this paper's maturity tiering, including the resource-equity motivation. The accurate relationship, stated rather than obscured: HAIRA specifies governance maturity; this paper specifies the instrumentation that maturity requires. HAIRA's levels describe what an organization at each stage does; the event record, collectors, and lenses describe what it runs. The two axes are complementary — HAIRA does not address collection methods, compliance catalog mapping, or the event schema, and this paper does not address organizational readiness assessment.
  • Certification programs (Joint Commission RUAIH, URAC) and the CHAI control catalogs — requirement layers, per §1.6. They are best read not as competitors but as demand generators: each certification an organization pursues is an additional lens over the same captured record, and the CHAI catalogs, being free and public, are the natural first healthcare-native lens derivations alongside HIPAA.

The unoccupied position is therefore: runtime AI workflow capture, in healthcare, mapped to multiple compliance control catalogs, at a cost structure reachable by organizations that will never certify. The mid-2026 certification wave strengthens rather than weakens this position — an organization now facing HIPAA obligations plus one or two voluntary certifications faces several overlapping monitoring specifications, and satisfying each separately is several projects, while capturing once and applying several lenses is one. The thesis required a world with multiple overlapping frameworks to be worth anything; that world arrived in May 2026. Whether the position is unoccupied because it is valuable and new, or because it is not viable, remains the question §7.1 attempts to separate.

7.4 The structural adoption obstacle

No organization is obligated to adopt this. That is the premise of the document — the gap exists precisely because obligation drives implementation — and it is simultaneously the primary obstacle to adoption. Compliance and safety expenditure in healthcare is overwhelmingly mandate-driven, which predicts that a voluntary control will diffuse slowly regardless of its merit.

Three quantifiable cost offsets exist, in descending order of how readily they can be measured: deferred certification expenditure for organizations facing SOC 2 or HITRUST requirements ($35K–$100K+); audit labor displaced by automated evidence production; and expected breach cost reduction against a $7.42M sector average. Only the first is measurable in advance; the second is measurable retrospectively; the third is an actuarial argument that requires incidence data this document does not have.

Consequence for the research agenda

The absence of incidence data linking control implementation to breach reduction is the most significant evidentiary gap in this entire document. The economic argument currently rests on the assumption that implementing controls without certification produces the security benefit of those controls. That assumption is plausible, widely held, and — as far as can be determined — unmeasured. See §9.1.

7.5 Sustainment models

Three structures could sustain the work, with materially different consequences for who benefits:

  • Open specification. The event schema and lens definitions published as a public standard, with implementation left open. Maximizes diffusion and citation; provides no funding mechanism.
  • Shared evaluation service. Maturity Tier S operated as a consortium or grant-funded utility. Aligns most closely with the equity argument; requires institutional sponsorship to reach viability.
  • Commercial tooling for regulated vendors. Serves organizations already purchasing compliance evidence. Financially the most tractable and the furthest from the population the equity argument concerns.

These are not exclusive, but they are not neutral either: the financially easiest path serves the population with the least need, which is the same dynamic §2.5 identifies in the problem itself.

7.6 Anticipated critiques

Each critique below is one a reviewer or implementer is likely to raise. The responses are arguments, not rebuttals, and two of them are partially conceded.

The EHR vendor already provides monitoring.

Partially conceded. Major EHR vendors do provide monitoring for models running inside their own products, and that coverage is real. Three properties are absent: visibility into AI outside that vendor's product, comparability across vendors, and independence — the party being evaluated supplies the evaluation. The first two are the substantive gaps; the third is a structural concern common to all self-reported quality data and is not unique to this domain.

This duplicates existing security event monitoring.

Partially conceded. Security information and event management systems collect overlapping data, and the Access and Boundary lenses are genuinely adjacent to their function. The distinction is in the question being asked: security monitoring asks whether access was authorized; these lenses ask whether an AI-influenced clinical decision was sound and whether the human response to it was appropriate. Where an organization already operates such a system, the architecturally correct choice is to emit into it rather than alongside it.

None of this is currently required.

Conceded, and it is the premise. The argument is that regulatory requirement is a lagging indicator of harm rather than a leading one, and that the marginal cost is low because the substrate is already funded. A secondary consideration: standards of care in this domain are actively forming, and organizations are evaluated against the standard prevailing at adjudication rather than at the time of the decision. That is an observation about liability exposure, not a claim about safety, and should not be substituted for one.

Monitoring will degrade clinical system performance.

Capture is asynchronous and the layer fails open: where an event cannot be written, the underlying call proceeds. This is a design commitment rather than a mitigation, and it is verifiable under test. It also has a cost — a fail-open layer cannot serve as a hard availability or access control, which bounds what the architecture can be relied upon for (§3.7).

No staff exist to operate it.

Maturity Tiers 1 and 2 at collector tier C0 require no analytic staff; proficiency testing and incident reporting are procedural rather than technical. Tier S externalizes the analytic function entirely. Where neither is available, the accurate conclusion is that the approach is not currently viable at that organization — which is a real limit on the equity claim in §2.5 and should be recorded as such rather than argued away.

Part 08

What would kill this

Written honestly, because a proposal that cannot name its own failure conditions has not been examined.

  1. EHR vendors ship it natively. The most likely death. They own the call path and the customer relationship. Countermeasure: independence and cross-vendor comparability are things they structurally cannot offer.
  2. Nobody buys a vitamin. Countermeasure: lead with the certification-deferral wedge, which has an existing budget line.
  3. The HIPAA Security Rule update lands and mandates something adjacent. This is arguably a tailwind rather than a risk, but it would reshape positioning overnight. Watch the July 2027 target.
  4. Legal blocks it on discoverability at enough organizations to stall the market. Countermeasure: response workflow as a first-class feature, and the three-part answer in Part 04.
  5. Log quality defeats the cheap tier. The C0 collectors depend on existing audit logs and message traffic actually being complete enough to answer a question. If they are not — and in many organizations they will not be — the entry tier collapses and the equity argument collapses with it. This is the most likely quiet failure and it should be tested first, not last.
  6. It becomes surveillance. One publicized case of individual clinician scoring would poison adoption across the field for years.
The test that matters

If an organization implements Tier 1 and Tier 2 and cannot answer a single question it could not answer before, the thesis is wrong. That is a cheap, fast, falsifiable test — run it before building anything further.

Part 09

Open questions and evidence base

9.1 Genuinely unresolved

  • How much volume is required before a change in human performance is detectable? This determines whether Tier S is optional or mandatory.
  • What is the minimum viable event schema? The version in Part 03 is a starting hypothesis, not a validated standard.
  • Can generative output be evaluated well enough to govern? Currently unsolved, and it is where most deployment is heading.
  • Does control implementation without certification actually reduce breach incidence? The premise is plausible and, to my knowledge, unmeasured.
  • Will organizations accept findings they are not required to act on?

9.2 Verify before publishing

Sourcing discipline

The figures in this document were gathered from secondary reporting and should be traced to primary sources before any of it is published or presented. Specifically: breach counts to the HHS OCR portal; the HIPAA Security Rule timeline to the Federal Register; certification costs to assessor firms directly, since they vary widely by scope; and appeal-overturn figures to the underlying CMS reporting rather than to analyses of it.
